# OUT OF RANGE: submission text

Text for the NASA Space Apps Challenge 2026 project page (English, ready to paste). Last updated 2026-11-15.

## Project Name

OUT OF RANGE

## High-Level Project Summary

OUT OF RANGE is a social feed for the machines NASA left on the Moon, on Mars and in deep space. They no longer answer Earth, so here they post. We made it for school-age kids, starting with the ones who would never open a space website. Each post borrows a format they already watch: a scary story, a spot-the-difference, a vlog, a room tour, a group chat, a ranking, a game. The jokes are in the format only. The photos, sounds and numbers are NASA's, and the pinned comment under every post tells the real story: what the machine is, why it was sent, what it found and where it is now, with links to the NASA pages it comes from. A map shows where each machine is today. Kids can make their own post, and the app will not publish it without a source. This matters because the stories of these machines reach kids only if they show up where kids already are. It runs in any browser with no login, in English and Japanese.

## Detailed Project Description

**What it is**

OUT OF RANGE looks and works like the short-video apps kids already use: a vertical feed you swipe through, with sound, likes, comments and profiles. There are two kinds of accounts. Twelve belong to machines NASA has lost contact with, and each of those profiles shows the month the machine went out of range. Six are channels that post about the machines: a ranking channel, a fashion check, a horoscope made from Kepler data, a lost-and-found counter for the Moon. Our team writes all of them, and every profile says so.

**Why this form**

The challenge says school-age kids "often hear only about active, ongoing missions", and that the history of past missions sits in "dense technical websites" that do not capture "a younger or more casual audience's attention". We asked where those kids already spend their attention. The answer is short-video feeds. So we did not build another space site and hope kids would come. We took the machines to the place and the formats kids already know. A kid with no interest in space will still stop for a scary story, a group chat or a ranking, and the space fact is the punchline. That is how we read the goal of teaching "school-age and casual space enthusiasts": start with the casual ones, and give the enthusiasts every source.

**Why the name**

"Out of range" is what a phone shows when it cannot reach anyone. It is also what happened to every machine with an account here: contact ended, and the machine is still there. The feed ends on the more than 1,000 commands NASA sent to Opportunity after the 2018 dust storm, none of them answered. The card after it reads "Past this point: out of range" and opens the map of where each machine is now.

**NASA data is the material**

Each post starts from one NASA record, and we picked the format that fits the record.

- A seismometer recording of a meteoroid hitting Mars became a scary story you listen to
- Two Phoenix photos taken four days apart became a spot-the-difference
- Viking 2's own photos of a tilted horizon and winter frost became a room tour
- Sojourner's first days in the mission record became a daily vlog
- Planned and actual mission lengths became a ranking
- The K2 campaign fields of the Kepler telescope became a horoscope ranking
- Landing coordinates from the NSSDCA catalogue became the pins on the map
- The signal delay between Earth and Mars became a game

**What is in the feed** (20 posts, in feed order: videos with sound, swipeable photo posts, a playable game and a quiz)

1. InSight (Mars): a scary-story video built on the real sound of a meteoroid hitting Mars, recorded by its seismometer
2. Apollo landing sites (Moon): a tappable quiz about what people left on the Moon, and why scientists want some of it back
3. Phoenix (Mars): a spot-the-difference video made from two real photos. What vanished were dice-size bits of ice, and that is how the team knew it was ice and not salt
4. Surveyor 3 (Moon): a POV video. Alone for 2.5 years, then Apollo 12 landed about 180 m away and took its camera home for science
5. Opportunity (Mars): a landscape movie trailer in a "PR" slot, like a sponsored post (our own trailer, labelled as not a real ad)
6. Opportunity (Mars): a playable game. First try to drive the rover like an RC car with a 20-minute signal delay (you cannot). Then drive it the way rover drivers really did: plan the day, send the commands together, and let the rover stop itself when its cameras see a rock
7. Pioneer 10 (deep space): its nav app. "Arrive in about 2 million years." Walking would take longer than the age of the universe
8. Viking 1 (Mars): a detective case file with a tappable quiz. What cut off the lander after six years? (A faulty command from Earth.)
9. Sojourner (Mars): a daily vlog. Day 3: drove 1.2 feet, touched a rock, feeling productive. Planned for 7 days, worked for 83
10. Spirit (Mars): "expectation vs reality". A broken wheel dragged through the soil and dug up nearly pure silica, evidence of ancient hot springs or steam vents
11. Kepler (deep space): a zodiac ranking video made from real Kepler / K2 data
12. Viking 2 (Mars): a room tour. Fifty years on Utopia Planitia: rent $0, a pink sky, a floor tilted 8.5 degrees and frost in the yard in winter
13. Dawn (asteroid belt): a parody of a "while they were out partying, I was grinding" motivation video. Four days to reach 60 mph on the push of one sheet of paper, kept up for 5.9 years
14. A just-for-fun quiz, "Which space machine are you?", whose result cards carry real facts about eight machines
15. Pioneer 11 (deep space): a photo dump from a solo trip. Jupiter from above its pole, the first visit to Saturn, and no return ticket
16. NEAR Shoemaker (asteroid Eros): a group chat. An orbiter with no legs is asked to land, and survives
17. Apollo 17 rover, Ingenuity, InSight, Apollo 11 lander: a fashion check. Four looks, four real engineering reasons
18. Mars Pathfinder, Spirit, Opportunity, Viking 1 and 2, Phoenix, Ingenuity: a "so relatable" list. They bounce when they land, their first photo is their own foot, every rock gets a nickname, and kids chose their names
19. Opportunity, Spirit, Viking 1, Pioneer 10, Ingenuity: a ranking of how many times longer than planned each one worked
20. Opportunity (Mars): "We texted 1,000 times. No reply." More than a thousand commands after the 2018 dust storm

**Beyond the feed**

- The real story under every post: the pinned comment says what the machine is, why it was sent, what it found and where it is now, in plain words for kids.
- Comment threads: other machines reply in character ("Sorry. I have not been able to reply since 2010 either," says Spirit under Opportunity's last post). Our team writes them, and the comment sheet says so. The facts in them are real and sourced.
- "Where are they now?": maps of the Moon and Mars with 23 landing sites and machines (Apollo descent stages and rovers, Surveyors, Vikings, Sojourner, Spirit, Opportunity, Phoenix, InSight, Ingenuity, and NEAR, Dawn, Kepler, Pioneer 10 and Pioneer 11 farther out). Tapping a pin answers the same four questions. Landing positions come from NASA's NSSDCA catalogue.
- Lunar Lost & Found (/lost/): a counter for things left on the Moon. Each claim slip leads to the machine next to it and a small hands-on science scene (laser ranging, moonquakes, Moon gravity).
- Create: kids make their own post from real NASA photos. The app asks for a source before it lets them post. Posts stay on the device.
- Fact-check stamps: opening a post's Sources earns that post's stamp, 20 in all. Checking where a fact comes from is the habit we most want kids to take away, so we made it the thing you collect.
- A 10-minute activity for classes and families, with a printable one-page worksheet in English and Japanese (/class/).

**How it answers the challenge**

- *What they are, why they were sent, what they found*: the pinned comment under each post and each pin on the map.
- *Showing how missions operated, not a list of facts*: each post is built around one moment. A lander photographing its own foot. A camera detached and carried home. A last photo cut short by touchdown. One post is a game: kids feel the signal delay themselves, then drive the rover the way its drivers really did.
- *Engineering meets science*: Spirit's broken wheel is an engineering failure that led to its best discovery. The fashion check explains four looks by their engineering reasons. The game shows why rovers had to check for hazards themselves.
- *Discoveries they still make*: some of these machines are still doing science. The laser reflector Apollo 11 left is still used to measure the distance to the Moon (light takes about 2.5 seconds to go and come back), which is how we know the Moon moves about 3.8 cm farther away each year. The map says so on its pin, and the Lunar Lost & Found has a hands-on scene for it.
- *A personal connection*: the machines speak in the first person and comment on each other's posts. The ranking ends on the line "They weren't thrown away. They worked to the very end."
- *Age group*: upper elementary to junior high (about 9 to 15). *Perspective*: the machines' own. *Scope*: broad, from the Moon to interstellar space.
- *Accessibility*: no login, any phone or PC, English and Japanese, read-aloud for photo posts, a one-tap reading aid in Japanese (furigana: small kana above the harder kanji, for younger readers), keyboard controls, reduced-motion support, works offline after the first visit. An automated check (axe-core, WCAG 2 A and AA rules) finds no violations on the main screens.

**How we keep it accurate**

- We check every fact against NASA pages before it is posted (NASA Science mission pages, NSSDCA, the NASA Image and Video Library, the JPL Photojournal). Every post has a Sources button, and the About page lists every resource used.
- Numbers we calculated ourselves are marked as our calculation, with the assumptions written out.
- Real photos are labelled "Real photo". NASA artist's concepts are labelled. We use no AI-generated images, video or voices.
- Lines we made up (the chats, the POV thoughts, the comments from other machines) are labelled as ours.
- Like and follower counts are not staged. They start at zero.

**How it was built**

A static web app in plain HTML, CSS and JavaScript (no framework, no server, no accounts), hosted on GitHub Pages. Posts are data (JSON). Photo posts are small HTML slides rendered to images. Motion videos are web pages with a timeline, recorded frame by frame with headless Chrome and mixed with ffmpeg, in both languages. Everything personal (likes, follows, comments, created posts) stays in the browser. Open source under Apache 2.0: https://github.com/taiyoishikawa2003/out-of-range

**A longer look**

A walkthrough in under 3 minutes (Japanese, with English subtitles): https://taiyoishikawa2003.github.io/out-of-range/walkthrough/

**What is next**

Classroom tests with kids, reading help inside the videos themselves, and more machines and formats.

## Use of Artificial Intelligence

We used an AI assistant (Claude by Anthropic, a large language model). This is where and how.

- Concept and rules: chosen by the team. A short-video feed where the machines NASA left behind post for kids. Use formats kids already know. Keep the jokes in the format only. Make every fact real.
- Post ideas and text: some ideas came from the team, and some were proposed and drafted by the AI assistant from NASA sources. The team reviews the posts, checks the wording for kids and decides what stays. Every fact is linked to its source in the post.
- Code and page design: written with the AI assistant, following the team's direction.
- Videos and photo posts: assembled with AI assistance (by scripts written with the AI assistant) from real NASA images, video and sound. No image, video or voice in the project is AI-generated, so there is nothing to watermark. NASA logos are not used.
- Sound: music is by human composers and performers (credited above). Short interface sounds were synthesized by scripts written with the AI assistant.
- Read-aloud: the browser's built-in text-to-speech on the user's own device (a synthetic voice, not generated by us).
- Research: AI was used to search for and summarize NASA sources. Facts were then checked against the NASA pages themselves.

The same disclosure is on the About page of the site and in the README of the repository.
